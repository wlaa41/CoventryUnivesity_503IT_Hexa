# Cybersecurity Questions for Adults (Ages 25+ / Professionals / Parents)

**Topic focus:** Work security, spear phishing, business email compromise, secure browsing, data protection, identity theft, AI-powered scams, compliance.

---

**1. The Urgent CEO Email**

*You receive an email from your "CEO" (the display name is correct, but the email address is ceo@company-secure.net, not the usual domain). It says: "I'm in a board meeting. Please buy $5,000 in gift cards and email me the codes. I'll reimburse you later."*

**What should you do?**

- A) Ask the CEO in person or call them on their known number.
- B) Buy the gift cards – the CEO is asking.
- C) Reply asking for more verification.

**Answer: A** – This is classic business email compromise (BEC). Always verify payment requests through a second channel.

---

**2. The USB Stick in the Car Park**

*You find a USB stick in the company parking lot labelled "Q4 Salary Reports – Confidential".*

**What should you do?**

- A) Plug it into your work computer to see what's inside.
- B) Hand it to IT security immediately – never plug in unknown USB devices.
- C) Take it home to check on a personal computer.

**Answer: B** – USB drops are a common attack vector for malware.

---

**3. The Multi-Factor Authentication Fatigue**

*Late at night, your phone buzzes with five Microsoft Authenticator approval requests in a row. You didn't try to log in.*

**What should you do?**

- A) Click "Deny" on all and change your password immediately.
- B) Click "Approve" once to see if it stops.
- C) Ignore them – they'll go away.

**Answer: A** – This is an MFA fatigue attack. The attacker hopes you'll accidentally approve. Deny and reset credentials.

**4. The AI Voice Call**

*You get a call from what sounds exactly like your daughter: "Dad, I crashed the car. I need $3,000 for repairs. Don't call the police. I'll send you a bank account number." The number is unknown.*

**What should you do?**

- A) Send the money – her voice is unmistakable.
- B) Hang up and call your daughter on her known phone number.
- C) Ask for a video call.

**Answer: B** – AI voice cloning is real. Always verify via a trusted number or in person.

---

**5. The "Your Bank Has Been Hacked" Text**

*You receive a text from "Lloyds Fraud Team" saying: "Unusual transaction of £1,200. Reply YES to block or NO to allow." It comes from a short code you don't recognise.*

**What should you do?**

- A) Reply NO immediately.
- B) Do not reply. Call the number on the back of your bank card.
- C) Click any link in the text for details.

**Answer: B** – Never use contact details from the suspicious message. Use the official source.

---

**6. The Public Wi-Fi Login**

*You're at an airport. The Wi-Fi network "Free_Airport_WiFi" has no password. You need to check your work email.*

**What's the safest action?**

- A) Connect and check email – it's convenient.
- B) Use a reputable VPN before accessing any sensitive account.
- C) Wait until you're on cellular data.

**Answer: B** – Public open WiFi can be spoofed or intercepted. VPN encrypts your traffic.

**7. The HR Payroll Change Email**

*HR sends an email: "We're updating our payroll system. Please log into this portal with your existing credentials to confirm your bank details." The link goes to hr-portal.companyname.net (a misspelling).*

**What should you do?**

- A) Log in – it looks official.
- B) Forward the email to your actual HR department via a new email.
- C) Click and check if the site has HTTPS.

**Answer: B** – Spear phishing targeting payroll changes is common. Verify with HR directly.

---

**8. The Smart Home Camera**

*You set up a cheap Wi-Fi camera to watch your front door. The app asks for "full network access" and "location always". You never changed the default password "admin".*

**What's the biggest risk?**

- A) The camera might break.
- B) Hackers could access the camera if default credentials are unchanged, potentially seeing inside your home.
- C) It will use too much data.

**Answer: B** – IoT devices are often poorly secured. Always change default passwords and limit permissions.

---

**9. The "You Owe HMRC" Phone Call**

*A recorded voice says: "This is HMRC. You owe £2,300 in back taxes. Press 1 to speak to an officer or we will issue a warrant for your arrest."*

**What should you do?**

- A) Press 1 immediately to resolve it.
- B) Hang up. HMRC never threatens arrest via robocall. Report it.
- C) Ask for a reference number.

**Answer: B** – Government agencies do not demand immediate payment by phone with threats of arrest.

---

**10. The Password Reuse Across Work and Personal**

*You use the same password for your work laptop, your personal Gmail, and a shopping site that was recently breached.*

**What is the consequence?**

- A) Low risk – they're different platforms.
- B) If the shopping site leaks passwords, attackers can try them on your work email.
- C) Only a problem if you use the same username.

**Answer: B** – Credential stuffing attacks use leaked passwords to break into other accounts. Never reuse work passwords.

---

**11. The Cloud Backup Scam**

*An email says: "Your Google Drive backup is corrupted. Click here to restore your files within 24 hours or they will be deleted." You do use Google Drive for work.*

**What should you do?**

- A) Click quickly – you need those files.
- B) Log into Google Drive directly via a browser (not the email link) to check.
- C) Forward to your IT department and wait.

**Answer: B** – Phishing preys on urgency. Verify storage status directly.

---

**12. The Social Media Quiz**

*A Facebook quiz asks: "What was your first pet's name? What street did you grow up on? What's your mother's maiden name?" These are common security questions for banking.*

**What should you do?**

- A) Answer – it's just for fun.
- B) Skip it – this is a social engineering tactic to collect password recovery answers.
- C) Answer with fake information.

**Answer: B** – Avoid giving real answers to security questions outside of official account recovery.

---

**13. The Unpatched Router**

*Your home router firmware hasn't been updated in three years. You get a notification that a new version is available, but you ignore it because "it's working fine."*

**What is the risk?**

- A) Minimal – routers don't need updates.
- B) Known vulnerabilities in old firmware can allow hackers to control your network, spy on traffic, or add your device to a botnet.
- C) Only affects speed.

**Answer: B** – Router firmware updates often contain critical security patches.

---

**14. The Deepfake Video Call**

*You're on a video call with your "CFO". The video looks slightly choppy, and they ask you to authorise a €50,000 transfer to a new supplier immediately. The voice sounds like them, but something feels off.*

**What should you do?**

- A) Authorise the transfer – the CFO is on video.
- B) End the call and confirm via a separate known communication channel (e.g., call their office direct line).
- C) Ask for a written email confirmation.

**Answer: B** – Deepfake video calls are emerging. Always verify financial requests through a second, trusted channel.

---

**15. The "Your Computer Has a Virus" Pop-up**

*While browsing a news site, a full-screen pop-up appears: "Windows Security Alert – Virus detected. Call 0800-XXX-XXXX immediately to renew your subscription." Your computer starts beeping.*

**What should you do?**

- A) Call the number and pay for removal.
- B) Force-close your browser (Task Manager or Ctrl+Alt+Delete), never call. Run your own antivirus.
- C) Restart the computer.

**Answer: B** – Tech support scams use fake alerts. Legitimate security software doesn't ask you to call a phone number.
